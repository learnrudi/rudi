Notes on AI for Teaching & Learning Nov 2025

How Colleges Can Help Faculty and Staff Learn to Use AI

Faculty and staff need practice, coaching, and support applying AI to their own teaching, research, and administrative work.

Finding a Specific Use for AI

Every tech tool that achieved mass adoption in education had a clear, one-sentence purpose that anyone could articulate:

  • Email: This is how I communicate.
  • Excel: This is how I organize and view data.
  • PowerPoint: This is how I present information.
  • Blackboard: This is where my course lives.
  • Zoom: This is how I meet with people remotely.

Does AI have this kind of sentence?

AI can help with writing, research, analysis, coding, and administrative tasks. Faculty and staff have to identify where it fits in their own work before they can judge whether it is useful.

That makes it harder to introduce through a single campus-wide use case.

A useful starting question is: which task am I doing repeatedly, and what would a better result look like?

Three Questions Leaders Are Trying to Answer

How do I keep up? Follow developments in your discipline and compare examples with colleagues. Focus on changes that affect the work you do.

How do I learn it? Practice on a recurring task, check the output, and revise your approach. Coaching and feedback help people work through problems that an introductory session cannot anticipate.

How do I integrate it? Choose a task, decide what information the system can use, and define who will review its output. That gives people a specific way to try it.

"So we don't get left behind" is a competition argument, but where's the use case? People don't adopt tools because of abstract competitive pressure. They need to see, concretely, what the tool does for their work.

And that's the fundamental problem. The institution can push. It can provide resources, subscriptions, training. But it can't hand someone a reason. The reason has to come from the individual understanding their own work well enough to see where AI fits.

• • •

The Catch-22

Which creates a catch-22.

There's a tempting idea: couldn't you use AI to figure out how to use AI? If you prompted a model with your school's mission, strategic plan, enrollment data, and curriculum structure, couldn't it help you strategize and implement itself?

And partially, yes. You can do that. You can feed a model your institutional context and get back a reasonable strategy document. It will suggest where AI fits into advising workflows, how to redesign syllabi, where to automate administrative bottlenecks. That output has real value.

But it breaks down in two critical places.

Implementation reaches individual workflows. A campus strategy still needs to be applied to a professor’s course preparation or an admissions team’s intake process. Someone has to understand those tasks, discuss concerns with the people doing them, and help test a workable approach.

People new to AI may need help framing the request. A faculty member can know their subject deeply and still be unsure what context to give a model or how to assess its response. A facilitator can help them make those decisions.

An experienced facilitator can work with faculty and staff to describe the task, supply relevant context, and evaluate the model’s suggestions. They need enough knowledge of both the institution and the tools to recognize when a proposed approach will cause problems.

• • •

We've Done This Before

Earlier campus technology changes offer examples of the staffing, training, and infrastructure adoption can require:

1980–1997
Computer Labs
Schools had to build physical infrastructure, hire lab coordinators, train faculty. Adoption took roughly 17 years and was entirely hardware-dependent.
1993–2005
Email in Higher Ed
Required network infrastructure, training, and a cultural shift from paper memos. Some faculty resisted well into the 2000s. About 12 years.
1995–2008
PowerPoint
Replaced overhead projectors. Required AV equipment in every classroom plus faculty comfort with the interface. About 13 years.
2000–2016
Learning Management Systems
Blackboard, then Canvas. Massive institutional lift: procurement, training, course migration. Many faculty were dragged into it. About 16 years.
2002–2020
Online Learning
Slow growth for 18 years, then COVID compressed the last mile into months.
2020
Zoom
The only example where adoption happened essentially overnight. The forcing function was a global pandemic.

These transitions required infrastructure, dedicated support people, training, and time. Those investments also matter when an institution introduces AI.

AI also creates a continuing need to evaluate generated output and revisit practices as the tools change.

Where the Parallels Break Down

Sending an email, uploading a course file, or joining a video meeting usually has a clear completion state. With generated text or analysis, the user must also decide whether the content is accurate, relevant, and suitable for the task.

That judgment takes practice.

People using the same ChatGPT service can get different results depending on the context they provide, the task, and how they review and revise the output. Model behavior also varies. Training should help users evaluate results and diagnose what needs to change.

And there's a second problem the historical parallels don't capture: AI doesn't stabilize. Email hasn't fundamentally changed in 25 years. Zoom is the same tool it was in 2020. But AI capabilities shift every few months. New models. New features. New limitations. So even if you train someone today, what you taught them may be partially obsolete by next semester. A one-time training model doesn't work when the tool keeps changing.

Using AI in teaching and research requires practice with framing questions, providing context, and checking results. Those skills develop as people apply the tool and receive feedback.

• • •

Learning the Interface and Learning to Apply AI

Institutions need to support both learning the interface and learning to use the system within a discipline. Each requires a different kind of help.

Learning the interface covers access, menus, settings, and basic operations. Onboarding helps people open the tool, find its features, and understand the institution’s rules for using it.

Learning to apply AI involves framing a problem, selecting source material, checking a response, and deciding what to do with it. Faculty and staff need examples from their field and time to practice on tasks they recognize.

Skills that provide useful comparisons include:

  • Google Search and information literacy. Same tool, wildly different outcomes. When the internet hit higher ed, two students with the same Google access could produce a research paper or a mess. That's why information literacy became a whole discipline. Librarians didn't just teach people where to search. They taught people how to think about searching.
  • Excel. Learning to build a financial model takes subject knowledge and practice as well as familiarity with the spreadsheet interface. A shared template can help, but users still need to understand and check the calculations.
  • Writing. Everyone has a word processor. Not everyone can write. The tool is identical. The output is entirely dependent on the operator's clarity of thought, their ability to structure an argument, their vocabulary.
  • Teaching itself. Faculty develop through practice, peer observation, and feedback on their courses. Teaching and learning centers support that development over time. AI learning can draw on those existing support structures.

When results depend on a person’s judgment and practice, institutions need to support that development over time. Shared guidance can establish a baseline, while coaching helps people apply it to their own work.

• • •

Practice, Coaching, and Peer Learning

How do faculty keep up with AI?

The same way they got better at teaching.

Provide regular practice, peer discussion, and help with current teaching and administrative tasks. Track whether people improve at evaluating output and completing those tasks.

Budgets need to cover the people who provide coaching, maintain examples, answer questions, and evaluate progress alongside the technology itself.

• • •

Who Will Own Faculty AI Development?

Most institutions don't have the infrastructure to do any of this. They don't have a way to measure whether faculty are getting better at using AI. They don't have people who can facilitate that development. And the people they'd need occupy an intersection that barely exists as a discipline yet, sitting between the technology and learning development.

IT handles tool adoption. The provost's office handles academic strategy. The Center for Teaching and Learning handles pedagogy. But AI fluency sits across all three, and none of them own it.

What's needed is a new institutional function: the equivalent of what teaching and learning centers became for pedagogy, built for AI. A person, a team, a resource center that can:

  • Design ongoing AI development programs for faculty and staff
  • Offer embedded support: office hours, one-on-one coaching, workflow consultations
  • Stay current as the technology evolves and translate those changes for the institution
  • Measure progress and adapt the approach
  • Champion AI literacy across every department, not as a mandate, but as a practice

Assign responsibility for faculty AI development and provide time and resources for it. An AI lead, shared resource center, and ongoing coaching can give faculty a dependable place to find help.

Plan Support While Faculty and Students Are Already Using AI

Look at the adoption curves for every prior educational technology. Each one had one of two things: a long runway (10 to 18 years of gradual institutional adoption) or a crisis that forced instant change, the way COVID did for Zoom and online learning.

Generative AI has neither. There's no 15-year window. The technology is moving too fast, the workforce is changing too fast, and students are already using these tools whether faculty are ready or not. But there's also no external crisis forcing everyone's hand overnight.

The Key Insight

Institutions can schedule practice, assign responsibility for support, and review progress now. That creates a way to respond as students’ use and faculty needs change.

• • •

Anticipating the Pushback

This argument will meet resistance. Here's where it's likely to come from, and how to think about it.

"We adopted Teams in six months. Why is this different?"

A rollout can teach people where to click. Applying AI to course design or research also requires them to select suitable inputs, check generated material, and decide whether it serves the task. Include those activities in training and evaluation.

"We don't have the budget for a new function."

Many institutions are already under-resourced. Asking a president who's struggling to keep the lights on to create a new institutional function is a legitimate tension. But the minimum viable version doesn't have to be a center. It could be one faculty fellow with a course release. It could be a shared resource across a consortium of schools. It could be a fractional AI lead who serves multiple institutions. The key is that somebody owns it.

"Can't the Center for Teaching and Learning handle this?"

In theory, yes. In practice, CTL staff themselves often don't have the technical fluency yet. That's the chicken-and-egg problem. The people who would logically own faculty AI development are in the same boat as the faculty they'd be supporting. This either means investing in CTL capacity first or bringing in someone from outside the traditional structure.

"The vendors will handle training."

Vendor onboarding can explain a tool’s interface and features. Faculty also need support applying it to their research methods, teaching goals, and institutional requirements. Campus staff or facilitators can work through those questions with them.

"You compared AI to teaching, but we're bad at faculty development too."

This is the sharpest pushback, and it comes from inside the house. If the model for AI skill development is pedagogy, and most institutions are honestly mediocre at ongoing pedagogical development, then the argument seems to advocate replicating something that doesn't work well. The answer is: this is an opportunity to build it better. The AI function doesn't have to inherit the limitations of existing faculty development. It can be leaner, more embedded, more responsive. But the acknowledgment is important. We're not pretending the existing model is perfect.

"Show me the ROI."

Funders want measurable outcomes. What changes when you invest in this? At minimum, the theory of measurement should include faculty AI fluency benchmarks, integration into course design, administrative efficiency gains, and student preparedness metrics. The field is young enough that hard data is scarce, but the directional case is clear: institutions that build this capacity now will be meaningfully ahead of those that wait.

"Students are ahead of faculty. Let them lead."

Some schools are already experimenting with student AI fellows who support faculty. It's cheaper, it builds student skills, and it addresses the gap immediately. This is a real option and should be part of the ecosystem. But it's not sufficient on its own. Institutional AI strategy can't depend on the most transient members of the community. Students graduate. The function has to be permanent.

"Won't the technology just get easier?"

Simpler interfaces can make it easier to begin. Users still need to choose appropriate tasks and judge the results against their subject knowledge and institutional requirements. Training should develop that judgment as well as teach the controls.

• • •

Build Ongoing Support for Faculty and Staff

Faculty and administrators need a place to bring questions about the AI tasks they are trying, the results they receive, and the rules they should follow.

An institution can provide that support through coaches, office hours, peer learning, and examples embedded in existing workflows. Assign an owner and review what people need as their use develops.

Institutions that document these practices and measure progress can share what works with other departments and campuses. That evidence gives leaders a firmer basis for deciding what to fund next.

Budget for the people who help faculty and staff use AI: coaches, program leads, and colleagues with time to share and evaluate their work.

• • •

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is AI adoption different from other technology adoption?

Using generative AI requires people to evaluate the content it produces as well as learn the interface. Task knowledge, instructions, source material, and model behavior all affect the result. Faculty and staff need practice checking and revising that output.

What support should follow an AI workshop?

Workshops can introduce a tool or skill. Follow them with practice on teaching, research, or administrative tasks, access to coaching, and opportunities to compare results with colleagues. Update guidance when tools or institutional requirements change.

What should institutions budget for alongside AI subscriptions?

Consider an AI program lead, coaching and office hours, workflow support, peer learning, and ways to assess progress. The scope can begin with a faculty fellow or a shared resource across institutions.

How do faculty keep up with AI?

Regular practice, discussion with colleagues, and support within existing teaching and research processes help faculty apply new developments. Set aside time for that work and review whether it is improving the tasks that matter.

Why do new AI users need help framing requests?

A person may know their discipline but be unfamiliar with what context an AI system needs or how its output can fail. Facilitators help them describe a task, supply suitable material, and evaluate the response.
