AI News for August 3, 2026

Delhi's High Court clears OpenAI in the ANI case while a German court finds Suno's music training infringed, the EU's Digital Omnibus enters into force beside the AI Act's new transparency duties, and American computer science enrollment falls as AI requirements spread to everyone else. All 72 stories from the day, below.

August 3, 202672 stories · 10 categories

Delhi’s High Court ruled that OpenAI’s use of ANI’s content for training did not infringe copyright, a precedent for every Indian publisher weighing a similar case. A German court answered the same question in the opposite direction: Suno infringed by training its music generator on protected songs, and a second account frames the decision as an order that Suno must license the copyrighted songs it trained on, reported as Europe’s first AI music ruling. Two jurisdictions, one question, opposite answers, the same day. The writers were not waiting on either bench — Charles Stross and John Scalzi denounced AI’s use of copyrighted material and the companies building AI into writing software. And the supply side did not pause for any of it: Alibaba unveiled Qwen3.8-Max, a 2.4-trillion-parameter model it says will be released openly next week.

The EU spent the day being administered rather than debated. The AI Act began enforcing transparency and oversight rules on chatbots, content labeling, and general-purpose models on August 2, and the Digital Omnibus entered into force alongside it, postponing certain high-risk deadlines while leaving transparency and general-purpose obligations on schedule. A legal analysis traces where those rules bite first: hiring and academic integrity, with AI-enabled cheating cases already producing rulings. Financial supervisors moved the same week — the EBA, EIOPA and ESMA issued a joint statement on July 31 calling for one risk-based approach to frontier-AI risk across the sector. American statehouses supply the contrast. Massachusetts lawmakers adjourned with their campaign-deepfake bills apparently dead, leaving the state without deepfake rules before the election, while New York senators went narrow and fast, introducing a bill to ban humanoid robots from classrooms after a New York Focus report.

The clearest numbers of the day came from enrollment. Computer science majors are down while universities expand AI education to students who are not studying computer science; the Los Angeles Times puts institutions to it, with VCU, Northwestern and Purdue launching AI programs and requirements for non-CS majors, and a third account reads the same shift as colleges moving to meet employer demand. What employers pay for shows up in PwC’s 2026 barometer, which reports a 62% wage premium on AI skills and a labor market splitting into two tracks — one firm’s survey, so read the premium as its measurement rather than a settled market fact. PwC’s own US chief executive, asked what he tells his kids about MBAs and consulting, lands on adaptable skills. The advice and the enrollment data point the same way: the AI course is becoming the general requirement, not the specialization.
