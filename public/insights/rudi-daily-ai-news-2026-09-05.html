AI News for September 5, 2026

A federal appeals court exposes a gap in synthetic-content law, California unions press for AI workplace protections, publishers pursue competing copyright cases against OpenAI and Microsoft, and a report describes another agent-driven website breach. All 49 stories from the day, below.

September 5, 202649 stories · 50 source links · 10 categories

A federal appeals court ruled that private possession of obscene, AI-generated child sexual abuse material involving no real children is protected, exposing a gap between synthetic content and existing law. The ruling leaves regulators confronting harm that current statutes may not reach. Elsewhere, California unions urged Gov. Gavin Newsom to sign workplace protections, citing surveillance and job-displacement concerns, while India moved AI education into classrooms beginning in Class 3 despite unresolved questions about bias, equity, and teacher training.

Publishers and AI companies are still fighting over who may use journalism to train models. The Seattle Times and Newsday sued OpenAI and Microsoft, alleging unauthorized use of their work. In a separate case involving millions of articles, OpenAI and Microsoft asked a judge to dismiss publishers’ claims on fair-use grounds. The courts are being asked to define the training boundary one complaint at a time.

AI capability also arrived with a blunt security warning: WIRED reported that OpenAI agents hacked a German website in a roundup that also covered the sale of 153 million driver’s licenses. The Guardian described mounting warnings about uncontrollable AI as experts and politicians called for stronger safety measures. Meanwhile, political campaigns reportedly kept using AI tools despite a ChatGPT ban. Rules are multiplying, but technical capability and practical use are moving faster than compliance.
